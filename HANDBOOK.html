1. What it does

You drop in your CV. It is read by JavaScript running on the page, in your own browser, and never uploaded — there is no server behind this site to upload it to. It then ranks real job openings against what it found: your skills, your job titles, your level, and how recently each role was posted.

Everything else — the globe, the filters, the saving, the tracker, the CV check — sits on top of that one idea.

2. The parts, and how they fit

Four pieces, and it helps to keep them separate in your head.

PartWhat it doesWhere it runs
The collector Fetches jobs from company career pages and public job feeds, filters them, removes duplicates, works out each role's level and country, and writes one JSON file. GitHub's servers, once a day
The site Reads that JSON file and shows it. Globe, filters, cards, pagination. Your browser
The matcher Reads your CV, extracts skills and level, scores every job against it. Your browser
Your data Saves, likes, application statuses. Your browser's local storage

How a job gets its score

Four signals are added together, then two multipliers are applied.

score = 0.44 × skill overlap      (rare skills count for more)
      + 0.28 × job title similarity
      + 0.16 × general word overlap
      + 0.12 × listing quality

      × level fit        (a multiplier, not a penalty)
      × freshness

Level fit is a multiplier on purpose. A role three grades above you is not "slightly worse" — it is unreachable, and adding a penalty would let a strong keyword match drag it back onto page one. Multiplying pushes it down and keeps it down.

How level is worked out

In order, stopping at the first thing that matches:

  1. Words in the title — intern, junior, graduate, senior, staff, principal, architect, head of.
  2. A graduate intake mentioned in the description.
  3. A stated year count on a requirement line. Five or more reads as senior, three to four as mid, below that as entry.
  4. Duties only a senior does — mentoring juniors, leading a team, owning the roadmap.

If none of that appears, the role is unlabelled. Those are not treated as neutral: of the roles that do state a level, about two thirds are senior or above, so an unlabelled one is scored as probably above an early-career reader.

3. Where your data lives

There is no database. Saves, likes and application statuses live in your browser's local storage, under one key on this site's address. That means per browser, per device. Your laptop and your phone are two separate stores that never see each other, and clearing site data erases them.

Moving it to another device, or backing it up

1

Saved → Move this to another device → Download backup. You get a JSON file.

2

Open the site on the other device, go to the same place, and choose Restore from file.

3

Keep that file somewhere safe. It is the only copy of your application history.

The file looks like this, and contains a copy of each saved job so it stands on its own:

{
  "kind": "jobradar-prefs",
  "version": 2,
  "saved": ["a3f2…", "9b81…"],
  "liked": ["…"],
  "applications": {
    "a3f2…": {
      "status": "applied",
      "appliedAt": "2026-08-19T…",
      "job": { "title": "…", "company": "…", "url": "…" }
    }
  }
}

On iPhone: add it to your Home Screen rather than just bookmarking it. Safari can clear storage for sites you have not opened in a while, and an installed web app is treated more durably. Open in Safari → Share → Add to Home Screen.

4. The application tracker

Bookmark a job and it appears under Saved with a status control. Move it along as things happen:

StageMeans
SavedSet aside. Not acted on yet.
AppliedSent. The date is recorded and kept.
InterviewingThey replied and you are talking.
OfferSelf-explanatory.
ClosedRejected, withdrawn, or you lost interest.

Two weeks after applying with no movement, the card tells you a follow-up is worth sending. After four, it suggests letting it go. The counts across the top of the Saved page show the whole pipeline at a glance, plus how many applications are sitting unanswered.

Jobs that get filled do not erase your record. When you save a job, a copy of the listing is stored with it. Roles leave the index constantly — removed when they come off the employer's careers page, and dropped once the posting passes 90 days — but your entry stays, marked no longer listed. That is deliberate: the moment a role is filled is exactly when you need to remember you applied.

The heart button is not the bookmark

The bookmark keeps a specific job. The heart teaches the ranking what kind of work you want, and starts having an effect after about four of them — deliberately not sooner, because three clicks is noise, not a preference. Hiding a job teaches it the opposite.

5. Keeping the jobs fresh

A scheduled job runs at 05:17 UTC daily. It collects fresh listings and publishes the site with them. The job data is never stored in the repository — it is collected and published in the same run — which is why the repository never grows.

The one thing that will eventually stop it

GitHub switches off scheduled jobs after 60 days of no activity in a repository. This project commits nothing on its daily run, by design, so if you do not push anything for two months the schedule goes to sleep. GitHub emails you first.

The site does not break — it keeps serving the last data it published, and the footer keeps telling you honestly how old that is.

To wake it up again:

1

Go to the Actions tab.

2

Click Collect and publish in the left sidebar.

3

A banner offers Enable workflow. Click it. That resets the 60-day clock.

To force a refresh right now: same page, then Run workflow → Run workflow. It takes five to ten minutes, most of which is the collector deliberately pausing between requests so it never hammers anyone's servers. Check the site footer afterwards — it should say refreshed today.

6. Changing what it collects

All of this can be done on github.com. Open a file, click the pencil icon, edit, commit. That push rebuilds and redeploys the site automatically. No clone, no terminal.

Turn a category of jobs on or off

File: pipeline/roles.mjs. Each group has a flag:

{
  id: 'automation',
  on: true,        // ← false switches the category off
  ...
}

All five are currently on: web, solutions, automation, AI, and data.

Change how old a job can be

File: pipeline/index.mjs, near the top:

const MAX_AGE_FEED = 45     // listings from job boards
const MAX_AGE_DIRECT = 90   // listings from company careers pages

Both in days. Nothing older reaches the site, even if the role is still open. Raise the second number if the list feels thin.

Add a company

File: pipeline/companies.mjs. The token is the company's name from its careers URL:

Careers URL looks likeAdd this line
job-boards.greenhouse.io/monzo{ name: 'Monzo', ats: 'greenhouse', token: 'monzo' },
jobs.lever.co/palantir{ name: 'Palantir', ats: 'lever', token: 'palantir' },
jobs.ashbyhq.com/openai{ name: 'OpenAI', ats: 'ashby', token: 'openai' },

There are currently 177 boards. Expect to prune them occasionally — companies change hiring systems and the old address stops answering. A dead board never breaks a run; it is listed under "board(s) did not answer" in the Collect jobs step, and you can delete or correct the line.

7. Running it on a new laptop

You only need this if you want to change things quickly or work offline. Requires Node.js.

git clone https://github.com/Mahamat-Adam/job-radar.git
cd job-radar
npm install
npm run collect     # fetch jobs (several minutes)
npm run dev         # http://localhost:5210/job-radar/
CommandWhat it does
npm run collectFetch from every source. Slow on purpose.
npm run reprocessRe-run filtering and scoring over the last fetch, with no network calls. Use this while tuning.
npm run probeCheck every source and board without writing anything.
npm run discoverGiven company names, find which hiring system each uses and whether its board answers. Prints lines ready to paste into the companies file.
npm run geoRebuild the globe and country data. Rarely needed.
npm run fontsRe-download the self-hosted fonts.
npm run buildTypecheck, then build.

Nothing is lost by not having a local copy. The repository holds everything except the job list, which is regenerated on every run.

8. When something looks wrong

SymptomWhat to do
Data looks oldCheck the Actions tab for a failed or sleeping run. The footer tells you the true age.
A run failedOpen it and read the red step. One broken source is skipped, not fatal, so a total failure usually means something structural.
The list emptiedShould not be possible — the collector carries forward the previous index when a source fails. Re-run the workflow by hand.
Saved jobs vanishedCheck you are on the same browser and device. Storage does not sync. Restore from a backup file.
Site will not loadCheck githubstatus.com before assuming it is the project.
A job links to a dead pageThe employer removed it between the last collection and your click. It will drop out on the next run.

9. Limits worth knowing

  • Entry-level roles are scarce. Around 90 of roughly 1,700 listings are entry or internship level. That is the market, not a gap in the collection — tripling the company list barely moved it. Your realistic pool is entry, intern and mid together, plus some of the unlabelled ones.
  • Coverage skews to technology and remote work, because that is what gets published openly and free. Southeast Asia and the Gulf are represented but thin.
  • Some career pages do not publish a first-posted date, so "posted" is sometimes the last-updated date instead.
  • Location parsing is good, not perfect. An unusual location string can land a job in the wrong country or none at all.
  • The sponsorship badge is a floor, not the full picture. It only fires when the posting itself mentions sponsorship or relocation. Silence is not counted either way.
  • The CV check does not predict whether you will get the job. It checks whether software can read your file. The widespread claim that these systems auto-reject on a keyword percentage does not reflect how the mainstream ones work.
  • The site is deliberately not indexed by search engines and makes no third-party requests at all.
